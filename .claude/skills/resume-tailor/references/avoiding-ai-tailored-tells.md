# Not sounding algorithmically tailored

This is a different failure mode from generic "sounds like AI wrote this"
(cv-translate-de's `native-german-style.md` and resume-summary-writer's
references already cover that — buzzwords, forced parallelism, stock
phrases). This file is about the tell that's specific to tailoring: a
resume that reads as having been mechanically matched to one particular
posting, which recruiters increasingly recognize on sight as AI-tool
output rather than a person's own resume.

## The named tells, most damaging first

1. **A suspiciously perfect match.** A recruiter's own stated heuristic:
   "if your resume is a 95-100% match [to the job description], that is an
   immediate red flag." Real experience almost never maps that cleanly onto
   a posting — some natural gap is expected and normal. Don't chase 100%
   coverage of the posting's terms; a fit table with one or two honest gaps
   reads as more credible, not less.
2. **Every required skill, in the posting's own order.** Matching the
   posting's own listed sequence exactly is a recognized fingerprint of
   "the JD was fed into a tailoring tool," distinct from just having the
   right skills. Reorder by what's actually most relevant/strongest for
   this candidate, not by mirroring the posting's own bullet order.
3. **Listing a required tool with zero context.** Naming a tool straight
   off the requirements list with no evidence of how it was used reads as
   copied from the posting, not demonstrated by the candidate. Every
   keyword added during tailoring needs to trace to something real the
   person actually did — see the fit table's evidence column.
4. **Mirroring adjectives instead of interpreting them.** If the posting
   says "collaborative" and the resume summary now says "collaborative
   team player," that's mimicry with no new information. "A bot mirrors, a
   human interprets" — show collaboration through what was actually done,
   don't restate the posting's own adjective back at it.
5. **Cross-bullet verb echo — the sharpest, most tailoring-specific tell.**
   When every bullet in a tailored resume suddenly uses the exact same verb
   the job posting used, that's traceable straight back to the posting's
   own text, not a generic AI word-bank. This is different from and worse
   than normal AI buzzword repetition, because it's checkable — a
   recruiter can hold the posting and the resume side by side and see the
   pattern. Vary verb choice across bullets the way a person naturally
   would, even when several bullets genuinely relate to the same posting
   requirement.
6. **Tone/seniority mismatch section to section.** When each section gets
   tailored independently against the same keyword list, the résumé can
   drift between register (a summary that suddenly sounds junior next to
   senior-sounding bullets, or vice versa). Read the whole document as one
   voice before delivering it, not as a set of independently-optimized
   blocks.

## The calibration rule

A commenter's rule of thumb on this, worth adopting directly: **roughly 80%
the candidate's real story, 20% targeted keyword alignment.** Tailoring
should feel like emphasis and honest rewording of a real resume, not a
rebuild around the posting's own vocabulary.

## What actually protects against all of this

None of these tells require an "AI detector" (none of the major ATS
platforms have reliable native AI-authorship detection — false-positive
rates against genuine writing are high enough that vendors themselves avoid
shipping it as an automatic filter). The real defense is upstream, in how
tailoring is done:

- Never add a skill/tool to the resume that isn't already true — the fit
  table's "missing and unsupported" bucket stays out of the resume, always.
- Reorder based on genuine relevance and strength, not posting order.
- Let natural gaps stay gaps. A fit table that's honest about a shortfall
  and a cover letter that addresses it briefly is stronger, and less
  detectable as gamed, than a resume massaged to look like it has zero
  gaps.
- Read the finished resume once as a whole, out loud, asking: would this
  read naturally to someone who has never seen the job posting? If a
  sentence would only make sense to someone holding the JD next to it, it's
  been over-tailored.
