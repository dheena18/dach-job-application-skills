# Writing a precise resume summary (core, market-general)

Sources: Novoresume's documented 4-part formula, a 23,191-resume empirical
analysis (Simplify.jobs), Indeed career advice, CMU Career Services,
The Muse, TopResume, Gergely Orosz's resume-writing principles (impact over
responsibilities). Cross-checked for convergence; single-source claims are
marked as such.

## The 4-part formula

Documented, consistent across sources:

1. **Identity + years + domain.** "AI Engineer with 2 years in production
   LLM systems", not "Experienced professional".
2. **Scope or specialization** — what specifically, within that identity,
   they've focused on or gotten good at.
3. **A quantified proof point.** A number the reader can't get from the job
   title alone: scale, impact, a result.
4. **Optional fourth line**: a second proof point, or a plain statement of
   what they're targeting next — stated as fact, not aspiration.

Empirical check: a 23,191-summary analysis found a median length of 64
words, 3-4 sentences — this formula produces roughly that length naturally
when each part is one sentence.

## Precise vs. vague — the actual mechanism

The difference is not vocabulary sophistication, it's **whether a claim has
evidence attached**:

- Vague: "Results-driven professional with a proven track record and
  excellent communication skills."
- Precise: "Cut checkout latency 38% by moving image processing to a
  background queue; the fix shipped in a week and needed no rollback."

Every sentence in a summary should pass one test: could this sentence be
true of thousands of other candidates with no edit? If yes, it's not
precise enough yet — either add a number, or cut it.

## Named mistakes, in order of how often they're flagged

1. **Buzzword adjectives with no evidence**: "results-driven", "dynamic",
   "team player", "detail-oriented", "seasoned", "hard-working",
   "passionate". Recruiters are documented as fatigued by these
   specifically because they're claims, not proof. If the trait is real, an
   achievement bullet already demonstrates it — the summary doesn't need to
   assert it separately.
2. **Narrative / storytelling register.** A resume summary is "the headline
   of your candidacy: short, measurable, tailored." A cover letter is
   "personal, narrative-driven, first-person voice." Content like "this is
   the part of the work I care about most" or "what I want to bring to a
   team" is cover-letter content in the wrong document — not because it's
   poorly written, but because it's the wrong genre for this slot. The same
   content has a legitimate home: cover letter, or a LinkedIn "About"
   section (which is explicitly a longer, first-person, narrative format by
   design — the summary block is not).
3. **Objective-statement framing** ("Seeking a challenging role where I can
   grow..."). For anyone beyond entry-level or an unusually nonlinear
   career change, this is dated. It centers what the candidate wants rather
   than what they've already proven, which is what a hiring manager is
   actually scanning for in the first five seconds.
4. **Restating the job title / duty list** with no result attached. If a
   sentence would be equally true before and after the person actually did
   the work, it's a duty description, not an achievement.
5. **First person pronouns** — a real but minor convention, not a hard
   rule. Roughly 6 in 10 professional summaries use zero personal pronouns
   (implied first person: "Product designer with six years..." not "I'm a
   product designer..."). Using "I" won't get a resume rejected; dropping it
   is a small, free polish signal, and reflective/emotional first-person
   content (point 2 above) is the actual problem, not the pronoun itself.

## Summary vs. objective — which one to write

For any candidate beyond entry-level: write a summary, not an objective.
An objective is forward-looking and candidate-focused (what I want). A
summary is proof-of-value-focused (what I've done, condensed for the
reader's benefit). The near-universal 2024-2026 guidance is that the
objective format is retained only for entry-level candidates, career
changers, or a background nonlinear enough that it needs explicit framing
before the rest of the resume makes sense.

## Length

40-80 words is the documented range that keeps a summary readable in the
5-10 second window a resume actually gets on a first pass. If the source
material (an existing draft, or the person's own account of their
background) runs longer, compress; don't pad a tight, accurate 40-word
summary out to hit a word count.
