# What actually makes a cover letter persuasive

Most of the widely-repeated "attention span" statistics for cover letters
don't hold up (the "7.4 seconds" figure comes from a resume eye-tracking
study, not letters, and gets misapplied constantly). Don't cite fake
precision in a letter or in reasoning about one. What does hold up:

## The CAR framework (Context, Action, Result)

A resume bullet compresses an achievement to fit one line. A cover letter
has room to say why it mattered and connect it forward to this employer.
Don't just restate the bullet — unpack it, then point it at the reader.

Worked example (invented, not from any real resume — replace with the
candidate's real, relevant achievement):

> Resume bullet: "Reduced onboarding time by 30%."
>
> Letter version: "New hires on my last team were taking three weeks to
> ship their first change, mostly because the setup docs were scattered
> across four tools. I rebuilt the onboarding flow around a single
> checklist tied to our actual CI pipeline, which cut that to two weeks.
> Your posting mentions a similar ramp-up problem for the platform team —
> that's the kind of gap I'd want to look at first."

Context (why it was slow), Action (what was actually done), Result (the
number), then a **forward connector** tying it to something specific the
posting or company actually said. The connector is what turns a listed
achievement into an argument for hiring this person for this job — leaving
it off is the most common way a strong achievement still reads flat.

## Pick 2-3 achievements, not more

Selection is by relevance to this posting's top requirements, not by which
achievements are most impressive in the abstract. A longer list of proof
points reads as padding, not thoroughness. If a fit table already exists
for this posting (from a `resume-tailor` pass), start there.

## Concreteness beats a list of qualities

Specific, verifiable, numeric claims reduce a reader's skepticism; vague
ones invite it. "I led a five-person team that shipped X in Q months" beats
"I have strong leadership skills" every time — this holds in both
languages and is one of the few genuinely well-supported findings here.

## Don't over-narrate a strong number

Wrapping an already-strong, already-specific result in extra scene-setting
can dilute it rather than help it — the number was doing the persuasive
work on its own. Save narrative texture (one vivid, specific detail) for
places where the underlying fact needs it, not for every achievement
mentioned. One vivid detail beats a dry list, but a story wrapped around a
strong metric that didn't need help just adds words.

## Genericness is the real memorability killer

A letter that could have been sent to twenty other companies unchanged is
the single most commonly named reason a letter gets ignored — not weak
formatting, not modest length. Every paragraph should contain at least one
thing that only fits this posting: a fact about the company, a phrase from
the posting's own actual requirements (used honestly, not mirrored
wholesale — see `resume-tailor`'s `avoiding-ai-tailored-tells.md` for what
over-mirroring looks like), or a specific detail about the role.

## Why this matters more now, not less

Tailored letters do get more callbacks than generic ones in field data.
But as AI-tailoring tools have become common, their correlation with
callbacks has been dropping — a letter that reads as competently
AI-shaped no longer differentiates the way it used to, because everyone's
letter now clears that bar. The target isn't "well-tailored," it's
"specific enough that it couldn't have come from a template." That's a
higher bar than keyword-matching the posting, not a lower one.
